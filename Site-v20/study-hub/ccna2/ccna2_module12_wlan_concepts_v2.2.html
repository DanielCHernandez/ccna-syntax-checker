12.1Introduction to Wireless▾

12.1.1 Benefits of Wireless

A WLAN (Wireless LAN) makes mobility possible within homes and business environments. Without running cable, users connect with laptops, tablets, and smartphones from anywhere within range.

Business benefits include cost savings when relocating employees within a building, reorganizing equipment or labs, or moving to temporary locations. A wireless infrastructure adapts rapidly to changing needs without the expense of re-cabling.

12.1.4 802.11 Standards

The IEEE 802.11 standards define how radio frequencies are used for wireless links. Newer standards use MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output) — multiple antennas on both transmitter and receiver to increase throughput. Up to 8 antennas supported.

🎯 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz — KNOW THE DIFFERENCE
2.4 GHz: Longer range, better through walls, more interference (microwaves, cordless phones). Standards: b/g/n/ax
5 GHz: Shorter range, faster speeds, far less interference, 24 non-overlapping channels. Standards: a/n/ac/ax

802.11a is NOT compatible with 802.11b/g — a is 5 GHz, b/g are 2.4 GHz. They cannot communicate with each other.

12.1.5 Radio Frequencies

WLAN networks operate in two specific bands of the radio spectrum:

  • 2.4 GHz (UHF) — used by 802.11b/g/n/ax — longer range, more interference
  • 5 GHz (SHF) — used by 802.11a/n/ac/ax — shorter range, faster, less congested

12.1.6 Wireless Standards Organizations

ITU-R
International Telecommunication Union — Radiocommunication Sector

Regulates the global allocation of the radio frequency spectrum and satellite orbits. Determines which frequencies are available for WLAN use.

IEEE
Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers

Defines the 802.11 standard — specifies how radio frequencies are modulated to carry data. Maintains the 802.3 Ethernet and 802.11 WLAN standards.

Wi-Fi Alliance
Global non-profit industry trade association

Certifies vendor products for 802.11 interoperability. Awards the "Wi-Fi Certified" designation — ensures devices from different manufacturers work together.

12.2WLAN Components▾

12.2.2 Wireless NICs

Wireless communication requires at minimum two devices: one with a wireless NIC (transmitter + receiver) and one network device (AP or wireless router). Most modern devices — laptops, tablets, smartphones — include integrated wireless NICs. Devices without one can use a USB wireless adapter.

💡
Most device antennas are embedded internally — you cannot see them on smartphones and laptops. They are not absent, just hidden inside the device casing.

12.2.3 Wireless Home Router

A home wireless router serves three roles in a single device:

  • Access Point — provides 802.11 wireless access and broadcasts the SSID
  • Switch — typically a 4-port Ethernet switch for wired devices
  • Router — provides the default gateway for internet access

Wi-Fi range extenders can extend coverage by connecting wirelessly to the router and re-broadcasting the signal — but a dedicated AP provides better performance.

12.2.4 Wireless Access Points

A dedicated AP provides wireless access independently of a router. Clients use their wireless NIC to discover nearby APs by their SSID, then associate and authenticate. After authentication, the client has access to network resources.

12.2.5 AP Categories

Autonomous AP
Standalone device — entire config stored on the AP itself.

Managed individually via CLI or GUI.

Use when: Only 1–2 APs needed. Each AP must be manually configured and managed.

Example: Home wireless router — a classic autonomous AP.
Controller-Based AP (LAP)
Lightweight AP (LAP) — no initial config needed.

Uses LWAPP to communicate with a WLC (Wireless LAN Controller).

Use when: Many APs needed — WLC automatically configures each new AP added.

WLC connects via LAG (Link Aggregation Group) — like EtherChannel, but WLC does NOT support PAgP or LACP.

12.3.3 BSS and ESS

TermWhat It IsIdentifierCoverage Area
BSS (Basic Service Set)A single AP and all its associated wireless clientsBSSID = MAC address of the AP — unique per deviceBSA (Basic Service Area)
ESS (Extended Service Set)Two or more BSSs joined through a common wired Distribution System (DS)SSID = human-readable name shared by all APs in the ESSESA (Extended Service Area)
🚨
SSID vs BSSID — most confused terms in Module 12.
SSID = the network name (e.g., "CiscoWiFi") — shared by ALL APs in an ESS. Clients see this name.
BSSID = the MAC address of ONE specific AP — unique per physical device. Clients don't see this directly.
In an ESS, many APs share one SSID but each has its own unique BSSID.
✅
Roaming within an ESS is seamless. When a client moves from one BSA to another within the same ESS (same SSID, wired DS connecting the APs), it can hand off without losing connectivity. This is how enterprise Wi-Fi allows walking between rooms without dropping the connection.

12.3.5 CSMA/CA

WLANs are half-duplex, shared media — only one device transmits at a time, and all devices share the same radio channel. A wireless client cannot detect collisions because it cannot hear while transmitting.

WLANs use CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance) — not CSMA/CD used by wired Ethernet:

📻 CSMA/CA Process — Six Steps
1
LISTEN
Client listens to the channel to see if it is idle — sensing no other traffic currently on the carrier (channel)
2
RTS
Client sends a Ready to Send (RTS) message to the AP requesting dedicated access to the network
3
CTS
AP replies with a Clear to Send (CTS) message granting the client permission to transmit
4
BACKOFF
If no CTS is received, client waits a random backoff time then restarts the process from Step 1
5
TRANSMIT
After receiving CTS, client transmits the data frame
6
ACK
All transmissions must be acknowledged (ACK). If no ACK received, a collision is assumed and the entire process restarts from Step 1
🎯 CSMA/CD vs CSMA/CA — KEY DISTINCTION
CSMA/CD (Collision Detection) = wired Ethernet — detects collisions after they happen, then retransmits.

CSMA/CA (Collision Avoidance) = wireless — tries to prevent collisions before they happen using the RTS/CTS handshake reservation system.

Wireless uses CA instead of CD because a transmitting wireless client cannot hear other transmissions at the same time — making collision detection impossible.

12.3.6 Wireless Client and AP Association

Three-stage process for a wireless client to connect:

  1. Discover a wireless AP — passive (receive beacons) or active (send probe requests)
  2. Authenticate with the AP — verify identity using the configured security method
  3. Associate with the AP — negotiate parameters and establish the connection

Parameters that must match for successful association:

ParameterDescription
SSIDNetwork name — must match; in enterprise, each SSID may map to a VLAN
PasswordPre-shared key required from the client to authenticate
Network mode802.11 standard — APs in mixed mode support multiple standards simultaneously
Security modeWEP, WPA, WPA2, or WPA3 — always enable the highest supported
Channel settingsFrequency band — can be automatic or manually configured

12.4CAPWAP Operation▾

12.4.2 Introduction to CAPWAP

CAPWAP (Control and Provisioning of Wireless Access Points) is an IEEE standard protocol enabling a WLC to manage multiple APs and WLANs. It encapsulates and forwards WLAN client traffic between APs and the WLC through tunnels.

CAPWAP FactDetail
Based onLWAPP — but adds DTLS security
TransportUDP — ports 5246 (control) and 5247 (data)
IP versionIPv4 by default (can use IPv6)
IP protocolsIPv4 = IP protocol 17; IPv6 = IP protocol 136

12.4.3 Split MAC Architecture

CAPWAP distributes MAC functions between the AP and the WLC — time-sensitive real-time tasks stay at the AP; management-level functions are centralized at the WLC:

AP MAC FunctionsWLC MAC Functions
Beacons and probe responsesAuthentication
Packet acknowledgements and retransmissionsAssociation and re-association of roaming clients
Frame queueing and packet prioritizationFrame translation to other protocols
MAC layer data encryption and decryptionTermination of 802.11 traffic on a wired interface
💡
The AP handles everything that needs to happen in real time (acknowledgements, beacons). The WLC handles everything that can be centralized (authentication, roaming, management). This split allows one WLC to manage hundreds of APs simultaneously.

12.4.4 DTLS Encryption

DTLS (Datagram Transport Layer Security) provides encryption between the AP and the WLC — prevents eavesdropping and MITM attacks on the management tunnel.

ChannelDTLS DefaultNotes
Control channel (CAPWAP management)✅ Enabled by defaultAll management traffic between AP and WLC is always encrypted
Data channel (WLAN client traffic)❌ Disabled by defaultOptional — requires a DTLS license on the WLC; when enabled, all client traffic is encrypted at the AP before forwarding to WLC

12.4.5 FlexConnect APs

FlexConnect enables branch office AP management from a corporate WLC over a WAN link — no local WLC needed at each branch.

ModeWLC StatusAP Behavior
Connected modeWLC reachableFull CAPWAP tunnel operational; WLC performs all management functions normally
Standalone modeWLC unreachableAP operates independently; authenticates clients locally; switches data traffic locally — branch stays operational even if WAN link fails
✅
Standalone mode is the key FlexConnect advantage. If the WAN link to the corporate office goes down, branch wireless clients can still authenticate and access local resources. Without FlexConnect, WAN failure = no wireless at the branch.

12.5.3 Plan a WLAN Deployment

Best practices for AP placement:

  • Note existing wiring and locations where APs cannot physically be placed
  • Identify interference sources: microwave ovens, cordless phones, baby monitors, fluorescent lights, motion detectors, wireless video cameras — all operate in or near 2.4 GHz
  • Position APs above obstructions
  • Position APs vertically near the ceiling in the center of each coverage area
  • Place APs where users gather — conference rooms are better than hallways
  • Use non-overlapping channels for adjacent APs (channels 1, 6, 11 for 2.4 GHz)
⚠️
Mixed-mode networks run at the speed of the slowest connected client. If an 802.11n AP also supports 802.11b/g clients, the presence of even one slower client forces the AP to accommodate that older standard — reducing overall network performance for all clients.
12.6WLAN Threats▾

12.6.2 Wireless Security Overview

A WLAN is open to anyone within range of an AP — an attacker does not need physical access to the building to capture wireless traffic. Attacks come from outsiders, disgruntled employees, and even accidentally from legitimate employees.

ThreatDescriptionMitigation
Interception of dataEavesdroppers capture wireless frames without encryptionEncrypt all wireless traffic — WPA2/WPA3
Wireless intrudersUnauthorized users attempt to access network resourcesStrong authentication (802.1X / WPA2 Enterprise)
DoS attacksInterference that disrupts or disables WLAN servicesUse 5 GHz; RF monitoring; WLC management
Rogue APsUnauthorized APs connected to the corporate networkWLC rogue AP detection; RF spectrum monitoring

12.6.3 DoS Attacks

Wireless DoS attacks result from three sources:

  • Improperly configured devices — admin error or intentional misconfiguration by an intruder with admin access
  • Malicious interference — attacker deliberately generates RF interference to prevent legitimate wireless communication
  • Accidental interference — microwave ovens, cordless phones, baby monitors, motion detectors, other 2.4 GHz devices
💡
The 2.4 GHz band is far more prone to accidental interference than the 5 GHz band — one major reason to prefer 5 GHz when higher speeds and reliability are needed.

12.6.4 Rogue Access Points

A rogue AP is an AP or wireless router connected to the corporate network without explicit authorization. Can be installed maliciously or by a well-intentioned employee wanting better coverage.

Once connected, a rogue AP can be used to:

  • Capture MAC addresses and data packets
  • Gain access to network resources behind the AP
  • Launch a man-in-the-middle attack

A user's personal smartphone hotspot also qualifies as a rogue AP if it circumvents corporate security — other devices sharing through it bypass the corporate security infrastructure entirely.

✅
Mitigation: Configure the WLC with rogue AP detection policies and use wireless management software to actively monitor the RF spectrum for unauthorized APs.

12.6.5 Man-in-the-Middle — The Evil Twin AP Attack

The most common wireless MITM attack uses an evil twin AP:

  1. Attacker sets up a rogue AP configured with the exact same SSID as a legitimate AP in a public location (airport, cafe, hotel)
  2. Clients near the rogue AP see a stronger signal and associate with the evil twin instead of the legitimate AP
  3. All client traffic flows to the rogue AP — attacker captures passwords, credentials, and data
  4. Rogue AP forwards traffic to the legitimate AP — return traffic is captured and forwarded back to the client
  5. Neither the client nor the legitimate AP is aware — the attacker reads everything silently
🚨
Open Wi-Fi environments are the primary target. Airports, cafes, and restaurants use open authentication with no PSK — making it trivial to impersonate the SSID. Any device that connects based purely on SSID name and signal strength is vulnerable. Mitigation: authenticate all wireless devices; WLC rogue AP detection; RF monitoring for duplicate SSIDs.
12.7Secure WLANs▾

12.7.2 SSID Cloaking and MAC Address Filtering

Two early security features still available on most APs — but both are weak and easily defeated:

FeatureHow It WorksWeakness
SSID CloakingDisables SSID broadcast in AP beacon frames. Clients must manually configure the SSID to connect.SSIDs are still discoverable using wireless scanners — probe requests and responses still reveal the SSID. Security by obscurity only.
MAC Address FilteringAdmin manually permits or denies clients based on their MAC address. Only listed MACs can associate.MAC addresses can be spoofed — attacker captures a valid MAC and impersonates it. Maintaining the list is also labor-intensive.
🚨
Neither SSID cloaking nor MAC address filtering are real security controls. Both are easily defeated by any attacker with basic tools. They are NOT substitutes for proper encryption and authentication. Always use WPA2 or WPA3.

12.7.3 Original 802.11 Authentication Methods

MethodDescriptionUse Case
Open system authenticationNo real authentication — any client can connectPublic Wi-Fi where security is the user's responsibility (they should use a VPN)
Shared key authenticationMechanisms including WEP, WPA, WPA2, WPA3 — authenticate and encrypt with a pre-shared passwordAll scenarios where network security is required

SEC Security Evolution: WEP → WPA → WPA2 → WPA3

Each generation addressed critical weaknesses in the previous standard:

WEP
RC4 static
❌ NEVER USE
Wired Equivalent Privacy — Original 802.11 spec. Uses RC4 encryption with a static key that never changes between packets. Trivially crackable in minutes with freely available tools. The key never rotates, so capturing enough traffic reveals the key completely.
WPA
TKIP
⚠️ DEPRECATED
Wi-Fi Protected Access — Introduced TKIP (Temporal Key Integrity Protocol) which changes the encryption key per packet. Addressed WEP's static key flaw. Also added a Message Integrity Check (MIC). Still uses RC4 under the hood — now considered deprecated and insecure.
WPA2
AES/CCMP
✅ RECOMMENDED
Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 — Uses AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) with CCMP (Counter Cipher Mode with Block Chaining Message Authentication Code Protocol). Currently the industry standard. AES is considered the strongest encryption method. Detects if encrypted or unencrypted bits have been altered. Use WPA2 when WPA3 is not available.
WPA3
SAE/192-bit
✅ BEST — USE IF AVAILABLE
Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 — Uses SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals) for personal networks. The PSK is never exposed during the handshake — prevents brute force attacks that captured and analyzed the WPA2 handshake offline. Enterprise mode requires a 192-bit cryptographic suite. Open networks use OWE encryption. IoT devices use DPP for onboarding.

12.7.5 Home vs Enterprise Authentication

ModeAuthenticationInfrastructure NeededBest For
WPA2 PersonalPre-shared key (PSK) — same password configured on AP and given to usersNo server requiredHome, small office — simple and convenient
WPA2 Enterprise802.1X / EAP — individual user credentials via RADIUS serverRequires RADIUS authentication server (e.g., Cisco ISE)Enterprise — per-user authentication, full AAA logging
💡
In WPA2 Personal, the "shared key" is the pre-shared password given to everyone. In WPA2 Enterprise, the "shared key" is between the AP and the RADIUS server only — end users never see or use it. Users authenticate with their individual 802.1X credentials.

12.7.7 Authentication in the Enterprise (RADIUS)

Enterprise WPA2 requires a RADIUS server. Three parameters must be configured on the AP:

ParameterValue
RADIUS server IPIP address of the authentication server
UDP ports1812 (authentication) and 1813 (accounting) — or legacy 1645/1646
Shared keyUsed between the AP and RADIUS server only — not given to end users

End users authenticate via 802.1X/EAP (Extensible Authentication Protocol) — a flexible framework that provides the actual authentication mechanism and negotiates a secure session key for WPA2 AES encryption.

12.7.8 WPA3 Features

WPA3-Personal
SAE — Simultaneous Authentication of Equals

Replaces WPA2's PSK handshake. The password is never exposed during the handshake — prevents brute force attacks. Even if an attacker captures the handshake, they cannot perform offline dictionary attacks. Specified in IEEE 802.11-2016.

WPA3-Enterprise
192-bit cryptographic suite (CNSA)

Still uses 802.1X/EAP but requires a minimum 192-bit cryptographic suite. Adheres to the Commercial National Security Algorithm (CNSA) Suite. Eliminates mixing of legacy security protocols. For high-security Wi-Fi networks.

Open Networks
OWE — Opportunistic Wireless Encryption

WPA2 open networks send traffic in clear text. WPA3 open networks use OWE to encrypt all traffic even without authentication. Users still don't need a password, but their traffic is protected from passive eavesdropping.

IoT Onboarding
DPP — Device Provisioning Protocol

Replaces the vulnerable WPS. Headless IoT devices have a hardcoded public key — usually as a QR code on the device. Admin scans the QR code to onboard the device automatically. No manual configuration needed. DPP will replace WPS over time.

🎯Common Exam Traps — Module 12▾
❌
Trap 1: SSID vs BSSID — the most confused terms in this module.
SSID = the human-readable network name shared by all APs in an ESS. BSSID = the MAC address of one specific AP — unique per device. Many APs share one SSID; each has its own BSSID.
❌
Trap 2: CSMA/CA — NOT CSMA/CD.
Wired Ethernet uses CSMA/CD (Collision Detection — detects after). Wireless uses CSMA/CA (Collision Avoidance — prevents before). The difference: wireless clients cannot detect collisions because they cannot hear while transmitting. The RTS/CTS reservation system is the avoidance mechanism.
❌
Trap 3: 2.4 GHz non-overlapping channels = 1, 6, and 11 only.
Only these three channels in North America don't overlap. All others interfere with adjacent channels. Adjacent APs must use channels 1, 6, and 11 to avoid mutual interference. The 5 GHz band has 24 non-overlapping channels and avoids this problem entirely.
❌
Trap 4: 802.11a is NOT compatible with 802.11b or 802.11g.
802.11a operates at 5 GHz. 802.11b/g operate at 2.4 GHz. They cannot communicate with each other because they use completely different frequency bands. 802.11n was the first standard to span both bands.
❌
Trap 5: WEP is completely broken — never the correct answer for security.
WEP uses a static RC4 key that never changes. It is trivially crackable in minutes. When the exam asks for a secure authentication method, WEP is always wrong. Never recommend WEP under any circumstance.
❌
Trap 6: WPA uses TKIP — WPA2 uses AES.
Always match encryption to the correct protocol. TKIP = WPA (deprecated). AES with CCMP = WPA2 (current standard). These are frequently reversed on the exam.
❌
Trap 7: CAPWAP ports are UDP 5246 and 5247.
Control channel = 5246. Data channel = 5247. These are frequently tested. CAPWAP is built on LWAPP but adds DTLS security. Default IP version is IPv4.
❌
Trap 8: DTLS is ON by default for CAPWAP control — OFF for data.
Management traffic between AP and WLC is always encrypted. Client data forwarded through the tunnel is NOT encrypted by default — requires a DTLS license and explicit enablement.
⚠️
Trap 9: Autonomous AP vs Lightweight AP (LAP).
Autonomous = standalone, full config on the device, home router is the classic example, good for 1-2 APs. Lightweight = no initial config, managed by WLC via CAPWAP, automatically configured, scales to hundreds of APs.
⚠️
Trap 10: SSID cloaking and MAC filtering are not real security.
SSID cloaking: SSIDs are still discoverable with a wireless scanner — probe requests reveal the SSID even without beacons. MAC filtering: MAC addresses are easily spoofed. Neither is a substitute for WPA2/WPA3 authentication and encryption.
⚠️
Trap 11: WPA3-Personal uses SAE — not PSK.
SAE is the key improvement over WPA2-Personal. The pre-shared password is never transmitted during the SAE handshake — preventing offline brute force attacks that could crack WPA2 PSK from a captured handshake.
⚠️
Trap 12: FlexConnect standalone mode keeps branch wireless up when WAN fails.
In connected mode, the WLC does everything. In standalone mode (WAN link down), the FlexConnect AP handles local authentication and data switching independently — branch stays operational.
⚠️
Trap 13: RADIUS ports for wireless enterprise — 1812 auth, 1813 accounting.
Same ports as wired 802.1X (Module 10). Alternative legacy ports: 1645 and 1646. Know both sets. The shared key between AP and RADIUS is NOT given to end users — it only authenticates the AP to the server.
⚠️
Trap 14: Mixed-mode networks run at the speed of the slowest client.
A single 802.11b client connecting to an 802.11n AP in mixed mode degrades performance for all other clients. When possible, configure APs for single-standard operation to maintain maximum throughput.
⚠️
Trap 15: BSS vs ESS and roaming.
BSS = one AP + its clients. ESS = multiple APs sharing the same SSID connected by a wired DS. Seamless roaming is only possible within an ESS — moving between BSSs in the same ESS maintains connectivity. Moving between different SSIDs breaks the connection.
✅
Module 12 quick-reference summary:

Non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channels: 1, 6, 11
