7.1Purpose of WANs▾

7.1.1 LANs vs WANs

FeatureLANWAN
Geographic scopeSmall — home, office, campusLarge — cities, countries, continents
PurposeInterconnect local devices and usersInterconnect remote users, networks, and sites
OwnershipOne organization or home userISPs, telecom, cable, satellite providers
CostNo usage fee (infrastructure only)Provided for a fee / subscription
SpeedHigh bandwidth (Ethernet, Wi-Fi)Low to high; varies over long distances

7.1.2 Private vs Public WANs

🔒 Private WAN
Dedicated to one customer
  • Guaranteed service level (SLA)
  • Consistent, dedicated bandwidth
  • Security — not shared with others
  • Examples: leased lines, MPLS, Metro Ethernet
🌐 Public WAN
Shared — via ISP / internet
  • Service levels and bandwidth may vary
  • Shared connections — no guaranteed security
  • Lower cost than private WAN
  • Examples: DSL, cable, cellular, satellite

7.1.3 WAN Logical Topologies

Physical WAN topology is complex and hidden from users. Logical topologies describe the virtual connections:

⟷
Point-to-Point

Dedicated circuit between exactly two endpoints. Appears as a direct physical link to the customer. Expensive when many sites are required.

🕸️
Hub-and-Spoke

One hub router shared by all spokes. All spoke-to-spoke traffic must traverse the hub. Single point of failure at the hub.

🔄
Dual-homed

Two hub routers connected to spokes. Provides redundancy and load balancing. More expensive and complex to configure than hub-and-spoke.

🔗
Fully Meshed

Every site connected to every other site via virtual circuits. Most fault-tolerant of all topologies. Most expensive.

⚬
Partially Meshed

Some — but not all — sites are directly interconnected. Balances cost vs redundancy. Some sites must route through others to communicate.

💡
Large networks typically deploy a combination of these topologies — e.g., fully meshed core with hub-and-spoke branches.

7.1.4 Carrier Connections — SLA and Redundancy

Organizations sign a SLA (Service Level Agreement) with a service provider outlining expected reliability and availability. The carrier owns and maintains the physical connection.

TypeLinksProvidersResult
Single-carrier1 link1 providerCarrier link AND provider = both single points of failure. Cheapest.
Dual-carrier2 links2 providers (diff. carriers)Redundancy + load balancing. Loses internet if ISP (not just link) fails. More expensive.

7.2WAN Operations▾

7.2.2 WANs in the OSI Model

Most WAN standards operate at OSI Layer 1 (Physical) and Layer 2 (Data Link) — not Layer 3.

Layer 1 — Physical (fiber standards)

⚡ Fiber-Optic WAN Standards
SDH
Synchronous Digital Hierarchy — global standard for transporting data over fiber-optic cable
SONET
Synchronous Optical Networking — North American equivalent of SDH. Functionally identical; often written as SONET/SDH
DWDM
Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing — multiplexes 80+ channels onto one fiber using different wavelengths of light. Each channel = up to 10 Gbps. Dramatically boosts SDH/SONET capacity without new fiber.
💡
SDH/SONET use a ring topology with redundant fiber paths — traffic can flow in both directions for resilience. DWDM is used in long-haul and submarine cable systems.

Layer 2 — Data Link (encapsulation protocols)

Current
Broadband (DSL / Cable) · Wireless · Ethernet WAN (Metro E) · MPLSModern protocols in widespread use today
Less used
PPP (Point-to-Point Protocol) · HDLC (High-Level Data Link Control)Still encountered; often seen on serial links
Legacy
Frame Relay · ATM (Asynchronous Transfer Mode)Largely replaced by Ethernet WAN and internet-based solutions

7.2.3 WAN Terminology

Specific terms describe the connection between a subscriber (customer) and the WAN service provider:

📡 WAN Physical Layer Terminology
DTE
Data Terminal Equipment — customer's router or host that connects the subscriber LAN to the WAN (via the DCE). Generates and terminates data.
DCE
Data Communications Equipment — modem, CSU/DSU, or optical converter. Provides the interface between the subscriber and the WAN cloud. Provides clocking on serial links.
CPE
Customer Premises Equipment — all DTE and DCE devices at the customer site. Owned or leased from the service provider.
POP
Point of Presence — where the subscriber physically connects to the service provider network.
Demarcation
Physical location separating CPE from provider equipment. Defines the responsibility boundary: inside = customer; outside = provider.
Local Loop
Also called the "last mile." Copper or fiber from the CPE to the Central Office (CO). Where most WAN bottlenecks and failures occur.
CO
Central Office — local service provider facility connecting CPE to the provider backbone.
Toll network
High-speed fiber, switches, and routers inside the WAN provider backbone.
Backbone
Large high-capacity networks interconnecting service providers. Tier-1 providers own the backbone. Also connected to internet service providers.
🎯 WAN DATA PATH — KNOW THIS ORDER
Source DTE → DCE → WAN cloud → DCE → Destination DTE

The DTE is always the customer device (router). The DCE is always the service provider interface device (modem, CSU/DSU). The demarcation point sits between the CPE and the provider's DCE.

7.2.4 WAN Devices

DeviceDescription
Voiceband ModemLegacy dial-up; converts digital ↔ analog; uses telephone lines; max ~56 kbps
DSL Modem / Cable ModemBroadband modems; connect to DTE (router) via Ethernet; DSL uses phone lines, cable uses coaxial
CSU/DSURequired for digital leased lines (T1/T3); provides signal integrity, error correction, frame conversion; can be a separate device or a router interface card
Optical ConverterConverts fiber-optic signals ↔ electrical pulses (optical fiber to copper media)
Wireless Router / APProvides wireless WAN connectivity; can include cellular modems
WAN Core DevicesHigh-speed routers and Layer 3 switches forming the WAN backbone; must support multiple high-speed telecom interfaces

7.2.5 Serial vs Parallel Communication

Serial Communication ✅ WAN
  • Bits transmitted sequentially on one channel
  • Works over long distances
  • No synchronization timing issues
  • Standard method for all WAN communication
Parallel Communication ❌ Not WAN
  • Multiple bits on multiple wires simultaneously
  • Theoretically 8× faster than serial
  • Synchronization degrades with distance
  • Limited to <8 meters — not viable for WAN
  • Used in data centers (short distances)

7.2.6–7.2.7 Circuit-Switched vs Packet-Switched

🔁 Circuit-Switched
Dedicated path before communication
  • Dedicated circuit established before any data flows
  • Entire fixed capacity held throughout session
  • Wasteful — capacity used even when idle
  • Not suited for data due to inefficiency
  • All communication uses the same path
  • Examples: PSTN (analog), ISDN (digital)
📦 Packet-Switched
Shared network — no dedicated path
  • Traffic segmented into packets routed over shared network
  • No circuit establishment required
  • Many pairs of nodes share the same channel
  • Less expensive and more flexible
  • Susceptible to latency (delay) and jitter
  • Examples: Ethernet WAN, MPLS, legacy Frame Relay, ATM
7.3Traditional WAN Connectivity▾

7.3.2 Leased Lines (Dedicated Point-to-Point)

Pre-established copper WAN path; customer pays a monthly lease fee for dedicated bandwidth:

SystemRegionT1 / E1 SpeedT3 / E3 Speed
T-carrierNorth AmericaT1 = 1.544 MbpsT3 = 43.7 Mbps
E-carrierEuropeE1 = 2.048 MbpsE3 = 34.368 Mbps

Leased Line Advantages

  • Simple — minimal expertise to install and maintain
  • Quality — dedicated capacity; no latency or jitter; ideal for VoIP and Video over IP
  • Availability — always-on permanent connection

Leased Line Disadvantages

  • Most expensive WAN option — cost grows with distance and number of sites
  • Limited flexibility — fixed bandwidth rarely matches actual needs; capacity changes require ISP site visit

7.3.3 Circuit-Switched Options (Legacy)

Legacy
PSTN — Public Switched Telephone NetworkUses voiceband modem; converts digital ↔ analog over telephone lines. Maximum 56 kbps. Last resort when no other WAN option exists.
Legacy
ISDN — Integrated Services Digital NetworkDigital circuit-switching over PSTN local loop. 45 kbps to 2.048 Mbps — higher than dialup. Most providers have discontinued this service.

7.3.4 Packet-Switched Options (Legacy)

Legacy
Frame Relay Layer 2 NBMA (Non-Broadcast Multi-Access) WAN. Single router interface connects to multiple sites via PVCs (Permanent Virtual Circuits). PVCs identified by DLCI (Data-Link Connection Identifier). Up to 4 Mbps. Replaced by Metro Ethernet and internet-based solutions.
Legacy
ATM — Asynchronous Transfer Mode Fixed 53-byte cells (5-byte header + 48-byte payload). Small fixed cells suit voice/video — not delayed waiting for large packets. ~20% more overhead than Frame Relay. Cell-based architecture. Replaced by Metro Ethernet and internet-based solutions.
⚠️
Frame Relay and ATM are legacy/deprecated. Both have been largely replaced by faster Metro Ethernet and internet-based VPN solutions. Know their key characteristics for the exam — but don't expect to configure them.
7.4Modern WAN Connectivity▾

7.4.2 Modern WAN Options Overview

CategoryTechnologiesKey Feature
Dedicated BroadbandDark fiber (unlit fiber)Most expensive but maximum flexibility, control, speed, security
Packet-SwitchedMetro Ethernet, MPLSCurrent standard for enterprise WAN; replacing legacy Frame Relay/ATM
Internet-Based BroadbandDSL, Cable, Wireless, FiberCombined with VPN for security; most cost-effective
💡
Dark fiber = fiber-optic cable that is installed but not yet "lit" (not in use). Can be leased or purchased from suppliers. Gives the organization full control of the physical layer but is typically more expensive than any other WAN option.

7.4.3 Ethernet WAN (Metro Ethernet)

Ethernet WAN uses fiber-optic cables to extend Ethernet well beyond LAN distances:

  • IEEE 1000BASE-LX — up to 5 km
  • IEEE 1000BASE-ZX — up to 70 km

Service names: Metropolitan Ethernet (Metro E) · Ethernet over MPLS (EoMPLS) · Virtual Private LAN Service (VPLS)

BenefitDetail
Reduced expensesOne infrastructure for data, voice, and video — eliminates expensive protocol conversion from TDM/Frame Relay
Easy integrationConnects directly to existing Ethernet LANs — no new protocols needed at the customer edge
Enhanced productivitySupports VoIP, video streaming, and hosted IP applications
✅
Ethernet WAN is now the primary replacement for legacy TDM, Frame Relay, and ATM links in metropolitan areas.

7.4.4 MPLS — Multiprotocol Label Switching

MPLS is a high-performance provider WAN routing technology that transports any protocol as payload — IPv4, IPv6, Ethernet, DSL, Cable, Frame Relay — without regard to access method.

MPLS Router Roles

CE
Customer EdgeCustomer's router. Connects to the PE router. Unaware of MPLS labels — just sends/receives IP packets normally.
PE
Provider EdgeAt the edge of the MPLS network. Adds labels to incoming packets from the CE (ingress). Removes labels from outgoing packets to the CE (egress).
P
Provider (Internal)Internal MPLS router. Never looks at the IP header — forwards based on label only. Very fast forwarding. Also called LSR (Label Switched Router).
🎯 MPLS LABEL OPERATION — KNOW THE FLOW
CE sends IP packet → ingress PE adds MPLS label between frame header and IP header →
P routers forward by label only (never check IP header) →
egress PE removes label → delivers normal IP packet to destination CE

MPLS also provides: QoS support · Traffic engineering · Redundancy · VPN services
7.5Internet-Based Connectivity▾

7.5.2–7.5.3 DSL Technology and Connections

High-speed, always-on connection using existing twisted-pair telephone lines. Popular for home users and enterprise teleworkers.

TypeAsymmetric (ADSL)Symmetric (SDSL)
Upload vs downloadDownload > Upload (typical home use)Equal speed both ways (business use)
IncludeADSL, ADSL2+SDSL
Best forGeneral internet browsing, streamingBusinesses hosting servers
💡
Key DSL components: DSL modem converts Ethernet signals (from customer devices) to DSL signals → transmitted to DSLAM (DSL Access Multiplexer) at the provider CO → DSLAM concentrates multiple DSL subscribers into one uplink. DSL loop must be < 5.46 km (3.39 miles) from the CO for guaranteed quality.
✅
DSL is NOT a shared medium — each subscriber has a direct connection to the DSLAM. Adding users does NOT degrade performance (unless the DSLAM's uplink saturates). This is a key advantage over cable.

7.5.4 DSL and PPPoE

ISPs use PPP (Point-to-Point Protocol) for DSL because it provides authentication, public IP assignment, and link-quality management. Since Ethernet doesn't natively support PPP, PPPoE (PPP over Ethernet) is used:

MethodWho Runs PPPoE?UsersInside Protection
Host PPPoE clientIndividual host deviceOne device onlyNo router — no inside network protection
Router PPPoE clientEdge routerMultiple internal devices share connectionYes — router protects the inside network
🚨
Router PPPoE client is the production-correct method. The host PPPoE client is legacy/simple — no protection for the inside network. When a router acts as PPPoE client, all internal hosts share the connection and are protected by the router's security features.

7.5.5 Cable Technology — HFC and DOCSIS

High-speed, always-on connection using coaxial cable from the cable company:

  • HFC (Hybrid Fiber-Coaxial) — fiber from provider headend to neighborhood optical node; coax from node to customer
  • DOCSIS — international standard for high-bandwidth data over cable systems
  • CMTS (Cable Modem Termination System) — at the provider headend; communicates with all cable modems
  • Optical node — converts optical signals (fiber) to RF signals (coax) at the neighborhood junction
⚠️
Cable is a shared medium. All subscribers on the same coaxial segment share available bandwidth. During peak hours (evening), speeds can degrade significantly as more users are active. This is a key disadvantage compared to DSL.

7.5.6 Optical Fiber — FTTx

FTTx (Fiber to the x) — describes how far fiber extends before switching to copper or coax:

FTTH
Fiber to the Home — fiber reaches the residence boundary. Highest bandwidth of all FTTx options. Uses PON or point-to-point Ethernet directly from provider CO.
FTTB
Fiber to the Building — fiber reaches the building boundary (e.g., basement). Final connection to individual units via copper, coax, or wireless inside the building.
FTTN
Fiber to the Node/Neighborhood — fiber reaches a neighborhood optical node. Final segment to the premise uses twisted pair or coaxial cable.
✅
FTTx delivers the highest bandwidth of all broadband options. FTTH provides the best performance since fiber reaches all the way to the home with no copper last segment.

7.5.7 Wireless Internet-Based Broadband

TechnologyKey Details
Municipal Wi-FiCity-deployed wireless mesh; free or low-cost; may need directional antenna; not available in most cities
Cellular (3G/4G/5G)Mobile data via cell towers; 4G LTE up to 450 Mbps down / 100 Mbps up; 5G = 100 Mbps–10 Gbps+; widely available
SatelliteGeosynchronous orbit at 35,786 km; 5–25 Mbps download; very high latency (~250–600ms RTT); used in rural/remote areas; requires clear sky view toward equator
WiMAX (IEEE 802.16)City-wide wireless up to 30-mile range; largely replaced by LTE for mobile and DSL/cable for fixed

7.5.8 VPN Technology

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates encrypted connections over the public internet — replacing dedicated leased lines with VPN tunnels:

VPN BenefitDetail
Cost savingsEliminates expensive dedicated WAN links and modem banks; uses existing internet infrastructure
SecurityAdvanced encryption and authentication protocols protect data from unauthorized access
ScalabilityEasy to add users without significant infrastructure investment
Broadband compatibleWorks over DSL, cable, wireless — takes advantage of home high-speed internet
VPN TypeWho Sets It UpClient Awareness
Site-to-site VPNConfigured on routers at both sitesClients are unaware — transparent encryption
Remote Access VPNUser-initiated from a hostUser runs VPN client software or uses HTTPS browser to connect

7.5.9 ISP Connectivity Options

🔌
Single-homed
1 link · 1 ISP

No redundancy. Both link and ISP are single points of failure. Cheapest option.

🔁
Dual-homed
2 links · Same ISP

Link redundancy and load balancing. Still loses internet if the ISP itself goes down.

🔀
Multihomed
1 link per ISP · 2 ISPs

Redundancy against ISP failure. Load balancing possible. More expensive.

🌐
Dual-multihomed
2 links per ISP · 2 ISPs

Maximum resilience. Redundant links AND redundant ISPs. Most expensive option.

💡
For mission-critical internet access, use at minimum multihomed (two different ISPs). Dual-homed to the same ISP protects against link failure — but not ISP failure.

7.5.10 Broadband Solution Comparison

DSL
Distance-sensitive — degrades >5.46 km from CO. Asymmetric upload/download. Not shared — adding users doesn't degrade speed.
Cable
Shared medium — speeds degrade during peak hours when many users are active on the same coaxial segment.
FTTH / FTTx
Highest bandwidth of all broadband options. Requires fiber installation to the home — not universally available.
Cellular
Coverage issues in some areas. Bandwidth varies by generation (3G/4G/5G) and number of users on the cell tower.
Municipal Wi-Fi
Not deployed in most cities. When available, it's a viable low-cost option. May need directional antenna.
Satellite
Expensive. Limited capacity per subscriber. Very high latency due to 35,786 km orbit altitude. Last resort — use only when no other option exists.

🎯Common Exam Traps — Module 7▾
❌
Trap 1: WAN standards = Layer 1 and Layer 2 — NOT Layer 3.
DSL, Frame Relay, MPLS, Ethernet WAN are Layer 1/2 technologies. IP routing is Layer 3 — but that is handled above the WAN technology, not defined by it.
❌
Trap 2: DTE = customer's router. DCE = modem/CSU/DSU (provider interface).
DTE (Data Terminal Equipment) is the customer device generating data. DCE (Data Communications Equipment) provides the WAN interface to the provider cloud and provides clocking on serial links. Data path: Source DTE → DCE → WAN cloud → DCE → Destination DTE.
❌
Trap 3: Demarcation point = where customer responsibility ends and provider responsibility begins.
Critical for troubleshooting. Problems inside the demarcation = customer's problem. Problems outside = provider's problem. It's typically a cabling junction box at the customer premises.
❌
Trap 4: DSL is NOT a shared medium — Cable IS a shared medium.
Each DSL subscriber has a dedicated direct connection to the DSLAM. Cable subscribers share coaxial bandwidth on the same neighborhood segment — speeds drop during peak hours. This is the most common DSL vs cable comparison question.
❌
Trap 5: Frame Relay PVCs are identified by DLCIs.
Frame Relay creates PVCs (Permanent Virtual Circuits) that carry voice and data. Each PVC is uniquely identified by a DLCI (Data-Link Connection Identifier). One physical interface can carry multiple PVCs to different remote sites.
❌
Trap 6: ATM uses fixed 53-byte cells (5 header + 48 payload).
Small fixed cells make ATM suited for delay-sensitive voice/video traffic — no large packets can delay a voice cell. But the 5-byte header = ~20% more overhead than Frame Relay. ATM and Frame Relay are both legacy — replaced by Metro Ethernet.
⚠️
Trap 7: MPLS P routers never look at the IP header — they use labels only.
CE (customer edge) → PE (adds label) → P routers (switch by label only) → PE (removes label) → CE. P routers are extremely fast because they never do IP routing lookups. Only PE routers need to understand IP addressing.
⚠️
Trap 8: DWDM multiplexes 80+ channels onto ONE fiber — each up to 10 Gbps.
Each wavelength of light carries a separate 10 Gbps channel. DWDM dramatically increases fiber capacity without installing new cable. Used in long-haul and submarine systems. Supports SDH and SONET standards.
⚠️
Trap 9: DSL distance limit = 5.46 km (3.39 miles) from the CO.
Beyond this distance, signal quality cannot be guaranteed. Rural customers far from the CO may not qualify for DSL, or may receive very low speeds. This is why cable or satellite may be the only option in some areas.
⚠️
Trap 10: Satellite has very high latency — 35,786 km to geosynchronous orbit.
Signal must travel ~35,786 km up and back down. Round-trip latency is approximately 250–600ms — far too high for real-time applications like VoIP or online gaming. Satellite is a last-resort option, used only when no terrestrial broadband is available.
⚠️
Trap 11: Hub-and-spoke has a single point of failure at the hub router.
All spoke-to-spoke traffic must traverse the hub. If the hub fails, all inter-spoke communication fails. Dual-homed topology adds a second hub for redundancy. Know which topologies provide redundancy: dual-homed, fully meshed, partially meshed (partial).
⚠️
Trap 12: Dual-homed ISP = same ISP, two links. Multihomed = two DIFFERENT ISPs.
Dual-homed to the same ISP protects against link failure but not ISP failure. Multihomed uses two different ISPs — if one ISP goes completely down, the other still provides internet access. For maximum resilience: dual-multihomed (two links to each of two ISPs).
⚠️
Trap 13: FTTx — the "x" tells you where the fiber stops.
FTTH = fiber to the Home (best — no copper last segment). FTTB = fiber to the Building (copper/coax inside). FTTN = fiber to the Node/Neighborhood (copper/coax from node to the premise). More fiber = higher bandwidth and less signal degradation.
⚠️
Trap 14: PPPoE is needed because Ethernet doesn't natively support PPP.
ISPs use PPP for DSL to authenticate subscribers and assign public IPs. DSL modems connect to the DTE (router) via Ethernet. Since Ethernet has no built-in PPP support, PPPoE encapsulates PPP inside Ethernet frames. Router PPPoE client = multiple devices share; host PPPoE client = one device only.
✅
